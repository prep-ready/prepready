A canned food stockpile makes sense because cans need no fridge and no cooking, and a sealed can keeps its quality for a long time: according to USDA FSIS, 2 to 5 years for meat, fish, beans and most vegetables, and 12 to 18 months for tomatoes and fruit. Buy mostly protein (fish, meat, beans), add vegetables, fruit and a few ready meals, keep them cool and dry, and eat them in normal meals on a first in, first out basis.

Ready.gov advises keeping at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food, and the European Commission asks households to be able to cope on their own for at least 72 hours. This guide covers which cans to choose, what the dates mean, and when a can must go in the bin without tasting. For the wider picture, start with our [emergency water and food supply](/en/guides/emergency-water-and-food-supply/) guide.

## Which canned foods to stockpile

A stockpile has to feed you when there is no power, no gas and no shopping. So the priority is food you can eat immediately, without heating. Ready.gov recommends canned foods and staples that need no refrigeration, cooking, water or special preparation.

| Type | Examples | Why it helps | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fish | tuna, mackerel, sardines, salmon | protein and fat, fine cold | fish in oil has more calories than in water or brine; eat the oil with bread or crackers |
| Meat | chicken, corned beef, ham, pâté | filling, good on crackers | often salty, which makes you thirsty |
| Beans and pulses | kidney beans, chickpeas, lentils, baked beans | plant protein and fibre, cheap | large cans are hard to finish in one sitting |
| Vegetables | corn, peas, carrots, beetroot, tomatoes | vitamins and variety | glass jars are heavier and break more easily than cans |
| Fruit | peaches, pineapple, fruit purée | quick energy, children like it | fruit and tomatoes keep for less time than meat |
| Ready meals | chilli, stew, soup, meatballs in sauce | a whole meal from one can | less pleasant cold, but edible |

Three practical rules:

1. **Buy what you already eat.** A can nobody at home likes will sit until its date runs out and then go in the bin.
2. **Choose small cans.** Without a fridge you cannot keep an opened can, so one can should make one meal.
3. **Count the water.** Salty meat and fish make you thirsty. The CDC recommends at least 1 gallon (about 3.8 litres) of water per person per day.

### Military rations and "long-life" cans

Some shops sell military-style rations or cans marketed for long storage. They often have longer dates and portions sized as a full meal, and they usually cost more than ordinary supermarket cans with similar contents. For safety and storage they follow exactly the same rules as any other can. Check the date printed on the actual pack, not the product description, and avoid unlabelled cans or ones with no visible date, maker or batch code. On a tight budget, a few multipacks of ordinary cans give you a similar supply for less.

## How long canned food lasts: high-acid vs low-acid

Every can carries a date, set by the manufacturer from testing, and that is your first guide. For cans stored in good conditions, USDA FSIS gives these quality ranges:

| Type of canned food | Examples | Best quality (USDA FSIS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-acid | tomatoes, fruit, pineapple, pickles, sauerkraut | 12 to 18 months |
| Low-acid | meat, poultry, fish, beans, corn, most soups (not tomato) | 2 to 5 years |

High-acid foods keep for less time because, as FSIS explains, natural chemicals in the food keep reacting with the container, which slowly changes taste, texture and nutritional value. FSIS also notes that canning has limits: cans do not keep food indefinitely.

## Best before vs use by

In the EU, Regulation 1169/2011 provides for two kinds of date, and many other countries use similar wording. EFSA, the European Food Safety Authority, sums it up: "use by" is about safety, "best before" is about quality.

| Label | What it covers | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Best before…" | quality | food is still safe after this date if the pack is intact and was stored well, but flavour, colour and texture may decline |
| "Use by…" | safety | used for highly perishable food; do not eat it after this date, even if it looks and smells fine |

Commercially sterilised cans almost always carry a best-before date, often only a month and year, or just a year.

In practice:

- A can a few months past its best-before date, with an intact, flat lid: you can eat it, but look at and smell the contents after opening.
- Chilled products with a use-by date (some pâtés or lightly preserved fish from the shop fridge) are not stockpile food. If it needs a fridge, it will not help in a blackout.
- Whatever the date, throw out any can with the warning signs below.

## When to throw a can away

A damaged can may have let bacteria in. The most dangerous risk is botulism. The WHO notes that home-canned, preserved or fermented foods are a common source, and that very high-temperature treatment such as commercial canning kills the spores, which ordinary boiling does not.

Throw the can away without tasting it if (USDA FSIS and CDC):

- **it is bulging or swollen** (a domed lid or base),
- **it is leaking** or sticky from a leak,
- **it is heavily rusted** (light surface rust that wipes off is not serious; rust that has eaten into the metal is),
- **it has a deep dent on a seam**, meaning the rim of the lid or the side seam. A small dent elsewhere on an otherwise sound can is fine, according to FSIS,
- **it spurts liquid or foam when opened**,
- **the food smells bad, is discoloured or mouldy**, or the liquid is cloudy where it should be clear.

For glass jars, discard any with a domed lid or cracked glass.

Do not open a suspect can to check, and never taste the contents. The CDC is clear: you cannot see, smell or taste botulinum toxin, and even a small taste can be deadly. Put the can in a sealed bag, throw it out, then wash your hands and the work surface.

**Botulism symptoms** usually start 12 to 36 hours after eating, according to the WHO, with a range of about 4 hours to 8 days. Look for severe tiredness, weakness and dizziness, then blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, a dry mouth and difficulty swallowing or speaking. Weakness spreads downwards from the head and can paralyse breathing. With these symptoms, call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department: the person needs hospital care and antitoxin.

## How to store canned food

Cans dislike damp (rust) and heat. USDA FSIS says temperatures below 85°F (about 29°C) are best and that temperatures over 100°F (about 38°C) harm canned goods; the risk of spoilage rises sharply with temperature.

Good places at home:

- a kitchen cupboard away from the oven and radiators,
- a pantry, hall closet or the space under a bed,
- a dry basement (in a damp one, cans rust quickly).

Bad places: above the cooker, next to a heater, on a sunny balcony, in a garage or attic that heats up in summer.

### Rotation: first in, first out (FIFO)

A stockpile that is never touched starts expiring within a year or two. The simplest method is FIFO:

1. Put new cans at the back of the shelf and move older ones to the front.
2. Write the date on the lid in large figures with a marker, so you do not have to hunt for it on the label.
3. Once a quarter, use the cans with the nearest dates in ordinary meals and buy replacements.
4. During each check, remove any dented, rusted or bulging cans.

## How many cans per person: an example week

There is no official "cans per person" figure. The plan below is an **example** to help you work out a shopping list. It assumes cans form the base of lunch and dinner, while breakfast and snacks come from crackers, cereal, nuts and dried fruit.

| Type | Example for 1 adult for 7 days |
| --- | --- |
| Fish | 4 small cans |
| Meat or pâté | 4 small cans |
| Ready meals | 3 cans or jars |
| Beans and pulses | 3 cans |
| Vegetables | 4 cans or jars |
| Fruit or purée | 3 cans or pouches |
| **Total** | **about 21 cans or jars** |

Adjust for your household: children eat less, people doing physical work or living through a cold winter eat more. Check calories on the labels; a useful benchmark is 2,000 kcal a day for an adult, the reference intake used on EU food labels.

## Eating canned food without cooking

Commercial cans are sterilised during production, so most can be eaten straight from the can. A few ways to make it better:

- fish or pâté on crackers or crispbread,
- a salad of beans, corn and tuna mixed in a bowl,
- a ready meal eaten cold with bread,
- canned fruit as dessert and the syrup as a drink.

If you can heat food, tip it into a pan first. **Never heat a sealed can** over a flame or in a fire: pressure can make it burst. Safe heating is covered in our guide to a [camping stove for emergencies](/en/guides/camping-stove-emergency/). A gas camping stove belongs outdoors; use one indoors only if the manufacturer allows it, and always with a window open.

When the power goes out, eat what is in the fridge and freezer before opening cans. Our guide to [food safety during a power outage](/en/guides/food-safety-power-outage/) explains the order, and [blackout preparedness](/en/guides/blackout-preparedness/) covers the rest of the kit.

## Do not forget the can opener

A can without an opener is a can you cannot eat. An electric opener is useless in a blackout, and a knife is an easy way to cut yourself. Ready.gov specifically lists a manual can opener and eating utensils.

- Keep **two manual can openers**: one in the kitchen, one with the stockpile or in your go-bag.
- Ring-pull cans are convenient, but the ring can snap off. You will still want an opener.
- For a bag, a small folding opener or a multitool with an opener is enough. See our [go-bag checklist](/en/guides/go-bag-checklist/) and the [72-hour bag checklist](/en/checklists/72-hour-bag/).
- Add spoons, bin bags and wipes: an opened can and its leftovers are best sealed in a bag straight away.

## Building up for a longer crisis

For a longer emergency the rules stay the same; only the scale changes. Start with the 3-day minimum, then add a few cans to your normal shopping each week until you reach two weeks or more. A supply built this way is easier to rotate, and you avoid one large purchase that will all expire at once. Add water, crackers, nuts and dried fruit, and keep lighter, compact food in your evacuation bag.
