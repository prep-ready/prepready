Most preparedness advice assumes a house with a garage, a basement and space for a generator. Renters have none of that, and they often move every year or two. The good news: the things that matter most in the first 72 hours are small, cheap and portable. So are the skills.

The European Commission's Preparedness Union Strategy (March 2025) asks households to be able to cope on their own for at least 72 hours. Ready.gov says the same in different words: water and food "for several days". Here is how to reach that in a rented flat.

## Start with a 72-hour plan, not a shopping list

Before buying anything, answer four questions:

1. Which risks are real where you live: power cuts, floods, heatwaves, storms, wildfire smoke, conflict?
2. Where would you go if you had to leave — a friend, family, a shelter — and how would you get there without a car?
3. How will you reach the people you live with if the mobile network is down?
4. Who in the building might need help: elderly neighbours, parents with babies?

Write the answers on one page and put a copy in your go-bag.

## The renter's kit: small, stackable, portable

| Item | Why | How much |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | drinking, cooking, hygiene | 1 gallon (3.8 l) per person per day, 3 days to start |
| Water filter or tablets | stretches your stored water | 1 per household |
| No-cook food | tinned fish, crackers, nut butter, bars | 3 days, food you already eat |
| Torch or headlamp + batteries | light without fire risk | 1 per person |
| Battery or hand-crank radio | official updates without internet | 1 per household |
| Power bank | phone and USB lights | fully charged, checked quarterly |
| First aid kit + your medication | continuity of care | at least a week of prescriptions |
| Cash | card terminals fail in blackouts | small notes |
| Copies of documents and lease | ID, insurance, tenancy | waterproof pouch |
| Go-bag | for when you have to leave | see our [72-hour checklist](/en/guides/go-bag-checklist/) |

Everything on this list moves with you when you change flats. That matters more for renters than any single product.

## Where to store it when you have no storage

- Under the bed: flat water packs, tinned food in a shallow box.
- Top of the wardrobe: blankets, sleeping bag, spare clothes.
- Inside a suitcase you use twice a year: the whole kit, ready to roll.
- By the front door: the go-bag, so everyone in the flat knows where it is.

Keep water away from radiators and direct sun, and write the purchase date on each pack. Rotate food by eating it and replacing it.

## Skills that matter more than gear

These cost nothing and go with you from flat to flat:

- Find the shut-offs. On moving day, locate the water valve, the gas valve and the electrical panel. Take a photo of each.
- Know two ways out. Walk both fire exits once. In a blackout, the stairwell may be pitch black.
- Learn basic first aid. A one-day course covers bleeding, burns, choking and CPR.
- Stay off the grid for an evening. Switch off the main breaker for two hours and see what you are missing.
- Save water fast. When the power goes, fill the bath and pots while there is still pressure. Many buildings pump water to upper floors electrically.
- Keep warm without power. Choose one room, close the doors, layer clothing, sleep in a sleeping bag.
- Know your alerts. Learn your country's siren signals and install the official warning app.

## Safety rules specific to flats

- Never run a fuel generator, barbecue or camping stove to heat a room. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills.
- Do not use the lift during storms or rolling blackouts.
- Keep the hallway and stairwell clear. They are the escape route for everyone.
- If your building has a basement or underground car park, find out whether residents can get in during an emergency. In Poland, for example, these spaces can serve as temporary shelter.

## Talk to your landlord and neighbours

Ask your landlord where the shut-offs are, whether the building has a shelter space, who to call out of hours, and whether you can keep a small store of water in a shared basement. Ask your neighbours for a phone number. In a real emergency, the people on your floor are your first responders.

Check your tenancy insurance too. Know what it covers for loss of food, water damage and temporary accommodation, and keep the policy number with your documents.

## One task for today

Buy a pack of water, put fresh batteries in a torch and find your water shut-off valve. That is most of the first day covered.
