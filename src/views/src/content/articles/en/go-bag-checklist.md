A go-bag is the bag you grab within minutes when you must leave home because of a fire, flood, chemical accident or other threat. Pack it to keep one person going for at least 72 hours: water, ready-to-eat food, a 7-day supply of medication, copies of documents, cash, light, a radio, a phone power bank, warm clothing and basic tools. Keep one bag per person by the door, and check it every six months.

This list combines guidance from [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/kit), the American Red Cross, the Government of Canada, the UK government and the Polish government's *Safety Guide*. Quantities are per adult unless stated otherwise.

## The core list

| Category | What to pack | Per person |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Bottled water + filter or purification tablets | 1.5–3 l in the bag, a way to treat the rest |
| Food | High-calorie, ready to eat: bars, nuts, dried fruit | 3 days |
| Meds & hygiene | Regular medication, first-aid kit, sanitiser, basic hygiene, moist wipes | 7 days of meds (Red Cross) |
| Documents & cash | Copies of ID and key documents, a USB stick with scans, cash in small notes | waterproof pouch |
| Light & comms | Flashlight, battery or hand-crank radio, charged phone, power bank, cables, spare batteries | 1 light each |
| Clothing & sleep | Seasonal layers, rain jacket, sturdy shoes, sleeping bag or warm blanket, emergency blanket | one change |
| Tools | Multi-tool, whistle, dust mask, lighter, bin bags, duct tape, printed local map | – |
| Personal | Family photo, small keepsake, a toy for a child | – |

## How much water and food: the numbers

Agencies differ on water, so it helps to know who says what:

| Source | Water per person per day | Covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ready.gov / FEMA, American Red Cross, CDC | 1 gallon (about 3.8 l) | drinking and sanitation |
| UK government | 3 l | drinking, plus extra for cooking and washing |
| Government of Canada | at least 2 l | the minimum for a 72-hour kit |

Three days at one gallon a day is over 11 litres, which weighs over 11 kg. Nobody carries that on foot. That is why the bag holds 1.5–3 litres and a way to make more water safe. The CDC notes that portable filters do not remove viruses, so after filtering you should also disinfect the water with tablets made for drinking water. Iodine and standard chlorine tablets do not kill *Cryptosporidium*; chlorine dioxide tablets do, if used as directed.

For food, Ready.gov and the Red Cross ask for non-perishable food that needs no cooking or special preparation. In a bag, pick light and calorie-dense items: bars, nuts, peanut butter in a squeeze pack, dried fruit, crackers. Avoid glass jars and big cans. If you do pack a can, pack a manual can opener with it.

## Kit for children, older adults and pets

The basic list fits a healthy adult. Everyone else needs something added. The Red Cross and the Government of Canada suggest:

**Babies and young children**
- formula, bottles, baby food, nappies and wipes,
- a change of clothes in the current size (children outgrow the spare set fast),
- games, a book or a small toy,
- a card with a parent's name and phone number in their pocket or bag.

Older children can carry a small bag of their own with water, a snack, a torch, a whistle and their contact card.

**Older adults and anyone with medical needs**
- glasses and contact lenses, hearing aids with extra batteries,
- a 7-day supply of medication and a written list of medicines, doses and allergies,
- syringes or other supplies, plus any mobility aid,
- copies of medical information and insurance details.

**Pets**
The Government of Canada recommends food and water for at least 72 hours: about 4 litres a day for an average dog and 1 litre a day for an average cat. Add:
- a carrier or crate, leash or harness,
- bowls and any pet medication with the dose written down,
- litter, a litter tray, bags and paper towels,
- a recent photo, vaccination records and your vet's number.

Most evacuation shelters accept only service animals, so plan where your pet could stay. See our [family emergency plan](/en/guides/family-emergency-plan/) for how to build this into your plan.

## Packing order

1. Bottom: the heaviest, least-used items (sleeping bag, spare food, change of clothes).
2. Middle, close to your back: water. Weight near the spine is the least tiring.
3. Top: first-aid kit, rain jacket, flashlight.
4. Outer pockets: documents, cash, phone, power bank, today's medication.

Keep the bag light enough to walk with for an hour or more. Water is the heaviest item, at about 1 kg per litre. Walk a few kilometres with the packed bag once to be sure, and move weight to someone else's bag if it is too much.

Ready.gov suggests packing items in airtight plastic bags inside the bag. It keeps documents, clothing and food dry if you are caught in rain or have to wade.

## Home kit, work kit and car kit

A go-bag is only one layer. Ready.gov suggests kits in three places:

- **Home:** the go-bag by the door, and a larger stock of water and food for sheltering in place. The Red Cross suggests 3 days of supplies for evacuation and 2 weeks for staying at home. Our [water and food supply guide](/en/guides/emergency-water-and-food-supply/) covers the home stock.
- **Work:** be ready to shelter there for at least 24 hours. Keep food, water, medicine and comfortable walking shoes in a small bag in your desk or locker.
- **Car:** supplies in case you are stranded.

The Government of Canada lists these for a car kit:

| Car kit item | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Water in plastic bottles | plastic will not break if frozen; replace every 6 months |
| Non-perishable food such as energy bars | |
| Blanket, extra clothing and shoes | |
| First aid kit with a seatbelt cutter | |
| Small shovel, scraper and snowbrush | |
| Wind-up flashlight and a whistle | |
| Road maps and a copy of your emergency plan | |
| In the boot: sand, salt or non-clumping cat litter, washer fluid, tow rope, jumper cables, fire extinguisher, warning light or flares | |

A car kit is useful, but do not make it your only go-bag. You may not be able to reach the car, or you may have to leave on foot.

## Seasonal swaps

Swap a few items twice a year. The Government of Canada suggests:

| Season | Add or check |
| --- | --- |
| Spring and summer | rain jacket, umbrella, waterproof footwear, sunscreen, hat, insect repellent |
| Autumn and winter | warm clothing, gloves, socks, hat, heat packs, extra batteries, ice grippers |

In hot weather, water matters even more. In cold weather, a sleeping bag or warm blanket per person is not optional.

## What people usually forget

- Prescription medication. Rotate packs: new ones to the cabinet, older ones to the bag.
- Glasses or contact lenses. An old pair in a case is enough.
- Charging without mains power. A power bank at 100%, checked every quarter.
- Water on the move. Bottles are heavy, so a squeeze filter or tablets matter more than another litre.
- Spare keys for home and car, as the Government of Canada and Red Cross list.
- Cash in small notes and coins. Card terminals and ATMs need power.
- Paper copies of phone numbers. The UK government suggests a written list of important numbers, because a flat phone is a locked address book.
- Kids, older relatives and pets. Everyone at home gets their own bag.

## Documents to copy

The Red Cross and the Government of Canada list the papers worth copying. Keep paper copies in a waterproof pouch and scans on a USB stick or in secure cloud storage:

- ID for every person (passport, ID card, driving licence),
- proof of address, lease or deed,
- insurance policies (home, contents, car, health),
- a list of medications, doses and what they are for,
- bank details and emergency contacts, including next of kin,
- your household emergency plan.

## Where to keep it

By the front door or in the hall cupboard, somewhere everyone at home knows. Ready.gov asks for a designated place that all family members know about. Not in the basement or behind boxes: you may not be able to reach it in an emergency.

## Keep it ready

Set two dates a year, for example when the clocks change, and go through the bag:

1. Check expiry dates on food, water and medication. Eat the food and replace it.
2. Charge the power bank and test the torch and radio.
3. Swap seasonal clothing and check children's sizes.
4. Update documents and phone numbers.
5. Replace anything you used.

Ready.gov also suggests reviewing the kit once a year as your household changes: a new baby, a new pet, a new medication.

## Before you buy anything

Start with what you already own. Most of the list is everyday stuff: a torch, old clothes, a first-aid kit, document copies. Buy only the gaps.
